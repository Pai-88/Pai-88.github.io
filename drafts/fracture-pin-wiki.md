# The fracture pin: what a child's elbow demands of it

*For a manager deciding whether to make K-wires and Steinmann pins locally. It explains what the body requires of the design, and which of those requirements cannot be changed.*

## 1. What a fracture pin does

A fracture pin is a stiff metal wire, 1 to 6 mm across, drilled through skin into bone so that it crosses a break and holds the pieces still while they heal (Figure 1). The thin ones, up to 3 mm, are [Kirschner wires](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kirschner_wire), or K-wires; the thicker ones are Steinmann pins, for larger bones and [traction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Traction_(orthopedics)). Figure 2 compares them. Both go in under live X-ray ([fluoroscopy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fluoroscopy)), stay for weeks, and come out in a clinic room.

The device is a cut and ground length of wire, which is why it suits local manufacture. The operation is described in the [AO Surgery Reference for children's distal humerus fractures](https://surgeryreference.aofoundation.org/orthopedic-trauma/pediatric-trauma/distal-humerus).

## 2. The bone it enters

The running example is a displaced [supracondylar fracture of the humerus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supracondylar_humerus_fracture), a break just above the elbow, in a child aged five to eight. [1] Figure 3 names the parts; [OpenStax's chapter on bone structure](https://openstax.org/books/anatomy-and-physiology-2e/pages/6-3-bone-structure) gives the background.

> **[Figure 3]**
> **Alt text:** Section of a child's lower humerus: cortex, spongy core, thin bone at the fracture, growth plate. Shows where a pin grips.
> **Caption:** Figure 3. A child's lower humerus in section, with the usual fracture line.
> **Credit:** Own drawing for this page, schematic.

### Hard shell, spongy core

Bone is two materials: a dense outer cortex and a spongy core of cancellous bone. Above the elbow the humerus divides into two pillars with only a thin plate of bone between them, and the fracture runs through that weak point. [1] A pin grips only in the two cortices it crosses, and lunges forward when the far one gives way.

### Periosteum

Bone is wrapped in [periosteum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Periosteum), a tough living membrane that supplies healing cells. [2] In children it is thick, so a broken bone often stays hinged on it. When it tears right through, the fracture is unstable in every direction. [1]

### Living bone and heat

Bone is populated by [osteocytes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Osteocyte), cells sealed inside the mineral. They have a heat limit: in rabbit bone, 47 °C for one minute significantly reduced new bone formation, and 44 °C did not. [3] Drilled dry into pig bone, trocar tips reached 129 °C and diamond tips 98 °C; [4] drilled K-wire tracks in rabbits lost their osteocytes. [5] Heat rises with drill speed and diameter, and irrigation is the best coolant. [6] Dead bone does not hold a pin.

### The growth plate

Near each end of a child's long bone is a disc of cartilage, the [growth plate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epiphyseal_plate) or physis, where the bone lengthens. The one at the elbow supplies only about a fifth of the humerus's length. [7] Because [remodelling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bone_remodeling) follows growth, this end corrects little after age four: [1] a fracture that heals crooked stays crooked. That is why it is pinned, not just cast. Pins must cross this plate, so they must not damage it.

### Healing has a clock

[Bone healing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bone_healing) runs in overlapping stages (Figure 4): inflammation, which peaks within a day and is over within a week; a soft cartilage callus; a hard bony callus; then remodelling, which starts at three to four weeks and takes years. It is faster in the young. [2] In this fracture the pin comes out at three weeks. [1] Too short a stay and the fracture shifts; too long and infection risk climbs.

### Child and adult bone

Children's bone has less mineral, lower stiffness and lower strength than adult bone, but bends further and absorbs more energy before breaking. [8] A thin smooth wire that holds position for three weeks suits it. Adult weight-bearing bone heals slower and loads an implant harder, so plates, screws or nails take over.

## 3. What the pin passes through

### Skin

Skin keeps bacteria out. A pin holds a hole in it open for weeks, giving bacteria a track to bone (Figure 5). Reported pin-track infection runs from 1 to over 25 per cent. Most is superficial and settles with antibiotics and pin removal, [1] but one child in a series of 622 developed [infection of bone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Osteomyelitis) and joint. [9] Skin is also where a pin hurts: a protruding end rubs it, which decides how the end is cut, bent and capped.

> **Alt text:** A pin passing through skin, fat, muscle, periosteum and bone, with a nerve and artery beyond. Shows each tissue it crosses.
> **Caption:** Figure 5. Each layer the pin crosses, from skin to the far side of the bone.

### Nerves and artery at the elbow

Three nerves and one artery run close to this bone (Figure 6). The [ulnar nerve](https://teachmeanatomy.info/upper-limb/nerves/ulnar-nerve/) lies behind the [medial epicondyle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medial_epicondyle_of_the_humerus), the bony bump on the inner elbow, where a medial pin enters. The median nerve and brachial artery cross the [front of the elbow](https://teachmeanatomy.info/upper-limb/areas/cubital-fossa/); the radial nerve runs on the outer side. The fracture itself injures a nerve in 6.5 to 19 per cent of displaced cases. [1] The pin must not add to that, yet a medial pin costs one extra ulnar nerve injury for every 28 children. [10]

### Muscle, tendon and joint

A pin through a tendon tethers it, so the joint cannot move and is stiff after removal. Joint [cartilage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyaline_cartilage) cannot repair itself; a pin entering the joint scores it and can carry infection in.

### Immune response

Within minutes, body proteins coat the metal. [Macrophages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Macrophage), the clean-up cells, cannot engulf a pin, so they fuse into giant cells on its surface and wall it off in a fibrous capsule: the [foreign body reaction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foreign_body_reaction). The surface decides how strong this is, most of all in the first two to four weeks, [11] the pin's whole stay. A scratched or corroding surface provokes more inflammation.

## 4. Why pins, and how well they work

### The injury

This is the commonest elbow fracture in children: 55 to 80 per cent of their elbow fractures, about 177 per 100,000 children a year, usually from a fall onto an outstretched hand. [1] The [Gartland classification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supracondylar_humerus_fracture#Classification) grades it (Figure 7). Types II and III are pinned.

> **[Figure 7]**
> **Alt text:** Gartland types I to III: fracture undisplaced, hinged, then fully displaced. Shows which fractures need pinning.
> **Caption:** Figure 7. Gartland types: I undisplaced, II hinged, III fully displaced.
> **Credit:** "Gartland Classification.jpg" by S. Benoudina, Wikimedia Commons, licensed CC BY-SA 4.0. Unchanged.

### Without a pin

Left unreduced, 26 per cent of type II fractures healed with [cubitus varus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cubitus_varus), the inward-bent "gunstock" arm. [1] A swollen elbow bent up in a cast also risks [compartment syndrome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compartment_syndrome), pressure cutting off the forearm's blood supply. Alternatives: a cast for type I, open surgery if the bones cannot be aligned from outside, plates in adults.

### Strengths and weaknesses

The strengths are plain: cheap, quick, and removed without an operation. The weaknesses show in a series of 622 pinned children: [9]

  - Pin migration needing a return to theatre: 1.8 per cent, the commonest.
  - Infection: 1.0 per cent, one case deep.
  - Position lost and re-pinned: 0.6 per cent.
  - Compartment syndrome: 0.5 per cent.

### Past, present and potential

The older layout crossed two pins, one from each side, because that is stiffer. Pooled trials price the trade: crossed pins lose position less often ([relative risk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Relative_risk) 0.70) but injure the ulnar nerve more (2.21). [12] Lateral-only pinning, wires spread wide at the fracture, is now the default; a series of 124 lost no reductions and injured no nerves. [13] Two improvements are within reach: a tip that runs cooler, [4] and a small incision for the medial pin, after which the extra nerve risk was no longer significant. [12]

### Who it varies with

Infection was 1.0 per cent in a US series [9] and 37.5 per cent with exposed wires in an Indian series of a neighbouring elbow fracture. [14] A three-year-old's elbow will remodel a little; a nine-year-old's will not. [1]

## 5. From body to design

| Scale | Body fact | Design choice | What a wrong pin does to the body |
|---|---|---|---|
| Macro | Small bone, thin between its two pillars | K-wires 0.8 to 3.0 mm; [15] 1.8 to 2.0 mm here [1] | Too thick, a large hole in thin bone; too thin, it bends |
| Macro | Larger bones and loads in adults | Steinmann pins to 6 mm; stiffness rises with diameter⁴ | An undersized pin bends and position is lost |
| Micro | Bone formation falls after 47 °C for one minute [3] | Tip shape sets heat: trocar 129 °C, diamond 98 °C, redesigned point 66 °C [4] | A ring of dead bone; fixation loosens [6] |
| Macro | Grip only where the pin crosses cortex | Two or three smooth wires spread wide [13] | Wires bunched together let the fragment twist |
| Macro | Growth plate lies in the pin's path | Smooth wire, never threaded [1] | A damaged plate grows crooked |
| Micro | Macrophages judge the surface in weeks two to four [11] | Passivated surface with no burrs or scratches [15] | Inflammation, loosening, pain |
| Macro | Nerves and artery beside the bone | A tip that needs little thrust; lateral entry | One extra ulnar nerve injury per 28 medial pins [10] |
| Macro | Skin held open for weeks | Length to bend and cap, or to bury | Pin-track infection, 1 to over 25 per cent [1] |
| Macro | Removal in clinic, child awake | Smooth shank, an end pliers can grip | A migrated pin means theatre: 1.8 per cent [9] |

### Where body and design conflict

**Stiffness against size**

Stiffness is cheap to buy with diameter, because it rises with the fourth power (the [second moment of area](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_moment_of_area)): 1.6 to 2.0 mm is 25 per cent wider but 2.4 times stiffer, and a 4.0 mm Steinmann pin is (4.0 ÷ 1.6)⁴ ≈ 39 times stiffer than a 1.6 mm K-wire. The body punishes diameter with a larger hole in thin bone and a tip nearer the nerves. Surgeons answer with two or three thin wires spread wide, [13] so the maker needs a range of sizes, not one.

**Cool against safe**

Pushing harder cools the bone, because a faster feed gives heat less time to build. [6] But thrust is what carries the tip through the far cortex towards the nerve and artery. The maker can ease both: in the pig-bone tests the redesigned point needed the least thrust and ran coolest. [4]

**Protruding against buried**

Wires left outside the skin come out in clinic with pliers; buried wires need a second operation. In a US series, exposed wires saved about $3,400 a patient. [16] Where pin-site care could not be assured, the Indian series found infection in 37.5 per cent of exposed wires against 17.5 per cent of buried ones. [14] The right answer depends on the country, so the product should allow either.

  
**What the maker must control**

  
    - Diameter: 0.8 to 3.0 mm K-wires, 1.8 to 2.0 mm for a child's elbow.
    - Strength: stainless wire at least 1,240 MPa up to 2.8 mm, 1,100 MPa to 4 mm, 960 MPa to 6 mm. [15]
    - Heat: bone below 47 °C at the instructed speed, with irrigation.
    - Surface: [passivated](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Passivation_(chemistry)), no burr or scratch visible to the naked eye. [15]
    - Stay: holds for three weeks, then out with hand pliers.
  

## 6. For a local manufacturer

The client's country is not named, only that it is not the UK. This page therefore assumes a setting: certified [316L stainless steel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SAE_316L_stainless_steel) bar is imported with mill certificates, hospitals can [steam-sterilise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Autoclave) but have no gamma or ethylene-oxide plant, and many children live a day's travel from the clinic, so removal visits get missed.

If you reverse-engineer an existing pin, copy four things exactly, because the biology above fixes them: the alloy ([ISO 5832-1](https://www.iso.org/search.html?q=ISO%205832-1)); the strength and finish ([ISO 5838](https://www.iso.org/standard/59183.html), the standard for pins and wires); the tip geometry; and single use under an [ISO 13485](https://www.iso.org/standard/59752.html) quality system.

Change freely what local conditions change. Supply non-sterile pins for hospital autoclaving, validated under [ISO 17665](https://www.iso.org/search.html?q=ISO%2017665). Leave wire long enough to bury. Add caps and a family leaflet written for long gaps between visits. Never substitute "surgical-looking" steel, and never resharpen.

## 7. Summary

A fracture pin is simple to make and hard to make right, because bone, nerves, skin and the immune system set limits that do not move. Copy the alloy, tip, surface and sizes; adapt sterilisation, packaging and the pin end to local care.

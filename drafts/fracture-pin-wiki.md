# The fracture pin: what a child's elbow demands of it

*For a manager deciding whether to make K-wires and Steinmann pins locally. It explains what the body requires of the design, and which of those requirements cannot be changed.*

## 1. What a fracture pin is and does

A fracture pin is a stiff metal wire, 1 to 6 mm across, pushed through the skin and drilled into bone so that it crosses a break and holds the pieces still while they heal. The thin ones (1.0 to 2.0 mm) are Kirschner wires, or K-wires. The thicker ones (2.5 to 6 mm) are Steinmann pins, for larger bones and traction. Both are placed under live X-ray, left in for weeks, and pulled out in a clinic room, usually without anaesthetic.

For a manager the attraction is plain: a cut and ground length of wire, minutes of theatre time, and nothing to cut out again later. The surgical steps are in the [AO Surgery Reference for paediatric distal humerus fractures](https://surgeryreference.aofoundation.org/orthopedic-trauma/pediatric-trauma/distal-humerus); this page does not repeat them.

## 2. The bone the pin goes into

The running example is a displaced supracondylar fracture of the humerus in a child aged 5 to 7: a break just above the elbow [1].

### Hard shell, spongy core

Bone is two materials: a dense, hard outer cortex and a spongy honeycomb inside, the cancellous bone. In a young child's distal humerus the cortex is a millimetre or so thick and most of the width is cancellous. A drill tip feels the cortex as resistance then a sudden give, and the cancellous bone as almost nothing. So a pin's grip comes from the two cortices it crosses, and a pin advanced carelessly plunges once the far cortex gives way (section 3).

### Periosteum

Bone is wrapped in periosteum, a tough living membrane that supplies healing cells. In children it is thick and loosely attached, which is why a child's fracture often stays hinged on it and heals fast: the callus grows from this sleeve. It also heals over small damage at the pin's entry point.

### Bone is alive and dies from heat

Bone is populated by osteocytes, cells in tiny chambers in the mineral, and they have a known heat limit. In rabbit bone, 47 °C for one minute reduced regeneration and 50 °C caused lasting damage [2]. A wire tip rubbing in bone passes this easily; temperature rises with drill speed, blunt tips and heavy pressure [3]. Dead bone does not grip the pin, so it loosens, and the dead ring is where infection takes hold.

### The growth plate

Near each end of a child's long bone is a disc of cartilage, the physis or growth plate, where lengthening happens. The distal humeral physis sits just below the usual fracture line and supplies about a fifth of the humerus's length [4]. Pins cross it routinely here, but damage from threads, heat or repeated passes can stop growth on one side and bend the arm. These injuries are graded by the [Salter–Harris classification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salter%E2%80%93Harris_fracture).

### Healing has a clock

Healing passes through four stages: clot and inflammation (days), soft cartilage callus (two to three weeks), hard bony callus (weeks to months), then remodelling (months to years) [5]. A six-year-old's supracondylar fracture is stable at about three weeks and pins come out at three to four. An adult long bone takes six to twelve weeks or longer. Dwell time must match this clock: too short and the fracture shifts, too long and infection risk climbs.

### Child bone against adult bone

Children's bone is less mineralised, holds more water and is markedly less stiff; it bends before it snaps, and it remodels so well that moderate malalignment corrects itself [6]. That is why a thin smooth wire, which only has to hold position for three weeks while fast-healing bone takes over, is a paediatric mainstay. In an adult weight-bearing bone, with slower healing and no remodelling to forgive error, smooth wires loosen, migrate and bend, so plates, screws or nails take over.

## 3. Everything the pin passes through or sits next to

### Skin: the barrier the pin breaks

Skin keeps bacteria out. A pin punches a hole in it and holds it open for weeks, giving bacteria a track straight to bone. The skin–pin junction is the weakest point of the whole treatment: every movement of skin against pin pumps surface bacteria downward. Pin-track infection is graded from redness that settles with antibiotics up to bone infection that forces removal [7].

### The nerves and artery around the elbow

Three nerves and one artery run close to the distal humerus. The ulnar nerve lies directly behind the medial epicondyle, the bony bump on the inner elbow, which is where a medial pin enters. The median nerve and brachial artery cross the front of the elbow, directly in front of the fracture. The radial nerve runs on the outer side. The fracture itself often injures these; the pin must not add to it. In a systematic review, about one child in 28 treated with a medial pin suffered an ulnar nerve injury that lateral-only pinning would have avoided [8].

### Muscle, tendon and joint surface

The pin crosses the triceps at the back or the forearm muscles at the sides. A pin through a tendon tethers it: the joint cannot move while the pin is in, and stiffness after removal is common. Joint surfaces are covered with cartilage that cannot repair itself; a pin entering the joint grinds it and can carry infection in.

### The immune response to a foreign body

Within minutes, body proteins coat the metal. Macrophages, the body's clean-up cells, arrive and, unable to engulf a pin, wall it off in a thin fibrous capsule [9]. This truce holds for weeks only if the metal releases no ions or particles. Corrosion products from poor steel or a scratched surface provoke inflammation, loosen the pin and cause pain.

### Nerves and pain

Skin and periosteum are rich in pain fibres; bone marrow is not. Most pin-site pain comes from skin rubbed by a protruding end and pressure on the periosteum. This decides how the pin is cut, bent and capped.

## 4. Why pins are needed and how well they work

### The injury

Supracondylar fractures make up about 3 per cent of all fractures in children and more than half of children's elbow fractures, peaking at 5 to 7 years, usually from a fall onto an outstretched hand [1]. The [Gartland classification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supracondylar_humerus_fracture) grades them: type I undisplaced, type II hinged, type III fully displaced. Types II and III are pinned.

### Without fixation

A displaced fracture held in a cast alone can slip. The elbow heals bent inward (cubitus varus, the "gunstock" arm), the growth plate may be damaged, and a swollen elbow inside a cast risks compartment syndrome, where pressure cuts off the forearm's blood supply.

### Alternatives

Cast alone for type I and some type II. Closed reduction and pinning for most type II and III. Open surgery when the fracture cannot be lined up from outside. Plates and screws in adolescents and adults. An external frame where the skin is badly damaged. Pins win on cost and theatre time.

### How well it works

Union is near universal in children; the problems are the complications [10].

- Pin-track infection: around 1 to 3 per 100, the commonest, usually settling with antibiotics and pin removal.
- Loss of position: a few per cent, mostly from pin placement and spread rather than the pin itself [11].
- Iatrogenic ulnar nerve injury: about 1 in 28 with a medial pin [8].
- Pin migration: smooth pins back out or push in, particularly if left long.
- Pin breakage: rare at 1.6 mm and above, more common with thinner wire.

### Population variability

Osteoporotic adult bone offers little cortical grip, so smooth pins loosen and a threaded pin or another implant is needed. Obesity adds soft-tissue depth, so a longer pin bends. A two-year-old's humerus takes 1.2 to 1.4 mm wire; a twelve-year-old's may need 2.0 mm. One size does not cover even one fracture type.

## 5. From body to design

Each row reads: a fact about the body, what it forces on the design, what the manufacturer must control.

| Body fact | Design consequence | Manufacturer must control |
|---|---|---|
| Small bone, thin cortex in a child | Diameter range 1.0 to 2.0 mm; stiffness rises with diameter to the fourth power, so 2.0 mm is 2.4 times stiffer than 1.6 mm | Tight diameter tolerance; a size range, not one size |
| Bigger, denser bone in adults and long bones | Steinmann pins 2.5 to 6 mm, threaded options for grip | The same tolerances across the wider range |
| Osteocytes die at 47 °C for one minute | Sharp trocar tip, which cuts cooler than a diamond tip [12]; low drill speed | Grind quality, single use, drill-speed instructions |
| Cortical grip, cancellous emptiness | Smooth shank in children for easy removal; thread where pull-out matters | Thread form, surface finish |
| Growth plate damaged by threads and heat | Smooth wire wherever a physis is crossed | Product line: smooth is the paediatric default |
| Weeks inside living tissue; corrosion and immune response | 316L stainless (cheap, stiff) or titanium alloy (MRI-friendly, costlier) | Alloy to ISO 5832-1 or 5832-3, passivation, clean sterile single-use pack |
| Ulnar nerve, median nerve and brachial artery within a centimetre of bone | A tip that does not skate; clear X-ray visibility; depth marks | Marking tolerance, radiodensity, resistance to bending |
| Skin breached for weeks | Length left outside, buried against protruding ends, caps, a clean cut end | Cap design, cutter specification, patient-facing instructions |
| Removal in clinic, child awake | Smooth surface, no bone ingrowth, an end a tool can grip | Surface finish specification |

### Where the body and the design pull against each other

**Stiffness against size.** Stiffness is cheap to buy with diameter: 1.6 to 2.0 mm is a 25 per cent wider wire but a 2.4 times stiffer one. The body punishes diameter. A wider wire needs a wider hole in a cortex a millimetre thick, can split a small fragment, and sits closer to the nerves. Surgeons resolve this with two or three thinner wires spread apart; the spread at the fracture matters more than thickness [11]. For the maker, the range matters more than the largest size.

**Sharp against safe.** A sharp trocar tip cuts cool and bites where it is placed, protecting osteocytes and growth plate. But it exits the far cortex without warning, into the zone of the ulnar nerve and brachial artery. A blunt tip would skate, heat the bone, then plunge harder. The surgeon manages this with low speed and by stopping at the far cortex; the maker's part is consistent sharpness, so the surgeon's feel stays reliable. A dull batch forces pressure, and pressure brings heat and plunge together. Single use follows: a resharpened pin has a tip nobody has measured.

**Protruding against buried.** Ends left outside the skin come out in clinic with pliers, no anaesthetic, no second operation, which matters where the clinic is a day away. But the protruding end is the infection pathway and rubs the skin. Buried ends are cleaner but need a second procedure, and the evidence does not strongly favour either on infection [10]. The design answer is to bend, cut short and cap the end, and to instruct the family. The product is not a wire; it is a wire, a cap, a cutter and a leaflet.

## 6. What this means for a local manufacturer

Assume certified 316L bar must be imported with mill certificates, hospitals can steam-autoclave but have no gamma or ethylene-oxide sterilisation, and many children live a day's travel from the fracture clinic, so pins stay in longer than planned and removal visits get missed.

Four things follow from the biology and cannot be negotiated. The alloy must be traceable to [ISO 5832-1](https://www.iso.org/search.html?q=ISO%205832-1) (stainless) or [ISO 5832-3](https://www.iso.org/search.html?q=ISO%205832-3) (titanium alloy). Every pin must be passivated. Sterility must be validated for the method actually available, here steam under [ISO 17665](https://www.iso.org/search.html?q=ISO%2017665). Pins are single use, sharp from the pack. All of it sits under an [ISO 13485](https://www.iso.org/search.html?q=ISO%2013485) quality system.

The legitimate adaptation is the packaging and sterilisation route: non-sterile pins for hospital autoclaving, with caps and a family leaflet designed for long gaps between visits. The forbidden one is the metal or the tip: no substitute "surgical-looking" steel, no resharpening.

## 7. Summary for the manager

A fracture pin is simple to make and hard to make right, because the bone, nerves, skin and immune system around it set limits that do not move. Those limits are certified alloy, measured sharpness, a size range and single use. Sterilisation and packaging can be adapted to local conditions.
